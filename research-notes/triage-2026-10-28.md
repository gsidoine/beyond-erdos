# Discovery triage — 2026-10-28

## Rejected as open: prime-sum matchings with distinct differences

**Problem considered.** Partition $\{1,\ldots,2n\}$ into pairs $\{a_i,b_i\}$ such that (i) all sums $a_i+b_i$ are prime and (ii) all $n$ absolute differences $|a_i-b_i|$ are distinct.

**Elementary theorem:** such a partition exists **if and only if** $2n+1$ is prime.

**Proof.** As each pair consists of distinct positive integers, its prime sum cannot be $2$; hence all pair sums are odd and each pair consists of one even and one odd number. All $n$ pair differences are therefore odd. Since the possible odd differences in $[1,2n]$ are precisely $1,3,\ldots,2n-1$, distinctness forces their use all at once. In particular, the difference $2n-1$ occurs, necessarily from the pair $\{1,2n\}$, whose sum $2n+1$ must be prime. Conversely, if $2n+1$ is prime, pair $i$ with $2n+1-i$ for $1\le i\le n$. Every sum is prime and the differences are $2n-1,2n-3,\ldots,1$. $\square$

**Decision:** do not add as a conjecture. The little theorem is not claimed novel. It illustrates why every appealing computational pattern should be tested for an elementary invariant before being promoted.
